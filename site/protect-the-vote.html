Election protection

Protect the vote in 2028.

American elections are run by counties and towns and staffed by ordinary neighbors. The strongest safeguard for 2028 isn't a court or a senator. It's enough trained, nonpartisan people at every table, in every county, from the first ballot to the final certification.

Find your role

Five ways to help

  • 01 · Official

    Become a poll worker

    Poll workers run the polling place: checking in voters, handing out ballots, closing up and counting. You're hired and trained by your local election office, and in most places you're paid. Requirements such as residency and registration vary by state.

    Find your local sign-up on EAC.gov →
  • 02 · Nonpartisan

    Monitor the polls

    The national, nonpartisan Election Protection coalition trains volunteers to help voters at polling places and report problems to its legal team. You don't need a law degree.

    Volunteer with Election Protection →
  • 03 · Legal

    Lawyers and law students

    Attorneys, paralegals and law students staff the 866-OUR-VOTE hotline, often in virtual shifts from home, and serve as legal observers on the ground.

    Sign up as a legal volunteer →
  • 04 · After the vote

    Watch the certification

    Counting doesn't end on election night. County canvass and certification meetings are usually open to the public. Show up, take quiet notes, thank the staff. Ask your county election office when and where they meet.

    Find your election office via Vote.gov →
  • 05 · Everyone

    Spread the hotline

    Make sure the people around you know where to call if something goes wrong when they vote. Report false or misleading election information online at reportdisinfo.org.

Leave the campaign at home

This page is on a pro-Ossoff site, but the work above is not campaigning. When you serve as a poll worker or nonpartisan monitor, you serve every voter, whoever they vote for.

  1. No campaign gear or talk. No Ossoff shirts, hats, buttons or stickers while you serve. Not a word about candidates.
  2. Follow the election officials. They run the room. Learn your state's rules in training and stick to them.
  3. Never confront, challenge or film voters. If something looks wrong, write it down and report it to the hotline or the official in charge.
  4. Stay calm and kind. Research shows that a partisan presence at polling places lowers voters' confidence. A calm, neutral one raises it.
  5. Know the difference. Poll watchers are a separate role: under state law they're appointed by parties or candidates. We don't appoint anyone. If you want that role, ask your county party.
